# Chapter 2 Study Guide: Nature of Insurance

Nov 22, 2026 · @Dobby

## Key terms

Insurance is the transfer of risk from one party to another through a legal contract. The insurer takes on the risk in exchange for premiums.

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Risk | Uncertainty regarding the possibility of loss |
| Pure risk | Only a chance of loss, no chance of gain. The only type that is insurable |
| Speculative risk | Chance of loss or gain. Not insurable |
| Risk pooling (loss sharing) | Spreading the cost of possible losses over a large number of similar (homogeneous) exposure units |
| Adverse selection | Higher-risk people seek or keep coverage more than lower-risk people. Also called selection against the insurer |
| Law of large numbers | The more similar, independent exposure units insured, the more closely actual losses match expected losses |
| Principle of indemnity | Restore the insured to the same financial position as before the loss, with no profit or gain |
| Peril | The immediate, specific event or cause of a loss |
| Named (specified) peril | Policy lists the perils it covers. Anything not listed is not covered |
| Open (special) peril | Policy covers all direct causes of loss except the perils it excludes |
| Loss | Unintended, unforeseen decrease in value due to a peril |
| Direct loss | Damage caused by a peril with no intervening cause. The peril is the proximate cause |
| Indirect (consequential) loss | A loss that results from a direct loss |
| Accident | Unforeseen, unexpected, unintended, sudden event at a specific time and place |
| Occurrence | Any event that causes a loss, including repeated or continuous exposure over time |
| Hazard | A condition that increases the chance a peril will occur |
| Physical hazard | Tangible condition you can see, measure or document (faulty wiring, high blood pressure) |
| Moral hazard | Dishonest character or intentional acts (lying on an application, fraud) |
| Morale hazard | Careless indifference because the insured has coverage (skipping preventive care) |

## Most-tested facts and Exam Tips

Every Exam Tip box in the chapter is collected here, grouped by topic. Exam Tip wording is marked **Exam Tip**.

### Risk pooling

- Four requirements: a large number of exposure units, similar risks, accidental and unintentional losses, and units independent of each other.
- Policyholders benefit by trading large financial uncertainty for a known premium. Insurers benefit by using statistics to predict losses and set premiums.
- **Exam Tip:** Risk pooling transfers risk from an individual to a group. The units must be similar (homogeneous). Insurers use risk pooling with the law of large numbers to predict losses and calculate premiums.

### Adverse selection

- It happens because applicants know more about their own risk than the insurer does. Example: waiting until you are ill to buy health coverage.
- Controls: medical underwriting (when permitted by law), waiting periods, preexisting condition limitations, complete and truthful applications, risk classification, higher premiums for higher risks, and group coverage.
- Warning signs: unusual urgency or incomplete information on the application, early claims or a pattern of claims, and coverage much higher than financial need.
- **Exam Tip:** Adverse selection increases the insurer's risk and causes higher claims costs than expected. Underwriting helps prevent it. Concealing material information on an application is a form of adverse selection.

### Law of large numbers

- Three conditions: independence, similarity, and a large number of units.
- **Exam Tip:** It helps insurers predict losses more accurately. Both the number AND the independence of units are crucial. It works together with risk pooling. Without it, insurers couldn't accurately price their products.
- **Exam Tip:** Large numbers alone are not enough. 1,000 homes in one neighborhood can all be hit by one flood, so they are not independent.

### Indemnity

- Accident, health, property and casualty contracts are contracts of indemnity (they reimburse the loss).
- Life insurance is a valued contract. It pays a set amount regardless of the actual loss.
- Exceptions to indemnity: life insurance, valued policies, replacement cost valuation.
- Watch for the words "make whole," "restore" and "same financial position." The principle that prevents insurance from being a source of profit is indemnity.

### Perils and loss

- Perils by line: accident and health covers illness, accidents, disease and disability (sudden or gradual onset). Life covers premature death. Annuities cover living too long. Property covers fire, windstorm, theft and so on. Liability covers legal responsibility to others.
- Named peril: only listed perils are covered (narrow). Open peril: everything is covered unless excluded. Comprehensive medical and standard life are open-peril examples.
- **Exam Tip:** Every accident is an occurrence, but not every occurrence is an accident. A car crash is an accident. A knee worn out over years is an occurrence.

### Hazards

- **Exam Tip:** Physical = "can be measured or observed." Moral = "intentional and dishonest" (like insurance fraud). Morale = "unintentional/careless because insured" (like skipping preventive care).
- Moral always involves dishonesty or intent. Morale is unintentional and comes from carelessness.

### Risk

- **Exam Tip:** Ask "Can this situation result in a gain?" If yes, it is speculative and not insurable. If no, it is pure and potentially insurable. Insurers only insure pure risks.
- Risk classes: standard (average risk, standard premium), substandard (higher risk: higher premium, lower benefit, or declined), preferred (lower risk, lower premium).

### Handling risk

- Avoidance is the most complete form of risk management. Reduction lowers the risk but doesn't eliminate it.
- Buying insurance is the best way to transfer risk. Reinsurance (one insurer transferring risk to others) protects insurers from catastrophic loss.
- Coinsurance (for example 80/20) is risk sharing. Deductibles are risk retention. Reciprocal insurers (inter-insurance exchanges) are risk sharing.
- Self-insurance is a planned strategy using reserves. No insurance is ignoring the risk, though choosing not to buy insurance still counts as retention.
- Loss prevention eliminates damage or loss: masonry instead of wood, removing flammables, de-icing wings.
- **Exam Tip:** Remember the methods with STARR: Sharing, Transfer, Avoidance, Reduction, Retention.

## Charts and memory hooks

### STARR: methods of handling risk

| Method | What it does | Examples | Best used when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **S**haring | Spreads risk among multiple parties | Coinsurance in major medical, reciprocal insurers | Multiple parties can share the risk |
| **T**ransfer | Moves risk to another party by legal contract | Buying insurance, incorporation, hold-harmless clauses, reinsurance | Large loss potential and transfer is cost effective |
| **A**voidance | Eliminates the activity or condition | Not building in a flood zone, not skydiving | Loss potential is severe and nothing else works |
| **R**eduction | Lowers likelihood or severity of loss | Smoke alarms, sprinklers, training, maintenance | Risk can't be avoided |
| **R**etention | Keeps the risk using your own reserves | Deductibles, self-insurance, no insurance | Losses are small, frequent and predictable |

The chapter summary also counts **prevention** as a sixth method (sharing, transfer, avoidance, reduction, retention, prevention).

### Hazards chart

| Hazard | Think | Life and health examples | Key point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Physical | "Can be measured or observed" | High blood pressure, obesity, mining job, skydiving, toxic materials | Seen or documented through exams or records |
| Moral | "Intentional and dishonest" | Lying on the application, faking symptoms, false claims, concealing preexisting conditions | Always involves dishonesty or intent |
| Morale | "Careless because insured" | Skipping preventive care, ignoring treatment plans, longer hospital stays than needed | Unintentional, stems from carelessness |

### Elements of an insurable risk

Hook: the loss must be **chance, measurable, predictable, not catastrophic**, the group must be **large**, and the premium must be **affordable**.

| Element | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Due to chance (accidental) | Outside the insured's control. Helps avoid adverse selection | Catching a cold |
| Definite and measurable | Time, place, amount and payability can be documented | Car accident at 2:00 p.m. Friday, $2,000 damage |
| Predictable | Average frequency and severity can be calculated | 18% of accidents involve distracted driving |
| Not catastrophic | Not too big or uncertain for the insurer | War, nuclear disaster, a $1 trillion life policy |
| Large number of units | Actuaries can apply the law of large numbers | Many homogeneous exposure units |
| Economically feasible premium | Affordable and small compared to the exposure | 45-year-old: $250,000 20-year term for under $500 a year |

### Law of large numbers: the three conditions

| Condition | Why it matters | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large number | More data points, better predictions | 100,000 homes beats 100 homes |
| Independence | One loss doesn't raise the odds of others | One house fire shouldn't spread risk to other insured homes |
| Similarity | Similar risks allow accurate prediction | Homes in one area built to similar standards |

### Pure vs. speculative risk

|  | Pure | Speculative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outcomes | Loss only | Loss or gain |
| Insurable? | Yes | No |
| Examples | Injury, illness, disability, death | Stock market, gambling |

## Review notes: what the course says to watch

**Distinctions to master**

- Moral = intentional/dishonest. Morale = careless/unintentional.
- Pure = loss only (insurable). Speculative = loss or gain (not insurable).
- Direct loss = immediate from the peril. Indirect loss = consequential.

**Common trick questions**

- Life insurance is a valued contract, not indemnity.
- Picking the right hazard type in a scenario.
- Accidents (sudden, specific time and place) vs. occurrences (can be gradual).
- Recognizing the right risk management method.

**When answering questions about...**

- Adverse selection: look for intentional concealment or urgency.
- Law of large numbers: all three conditions must be met (independence, similarity, large number).
- Risk pooling: focus on homogeneous exposure units.
- Handling risk: remember STARR.

**Essential definitions**

- Risk = uncertainty of loss. Peril = cause of loss. Hazard = condition increasing the likelihood of loss. Loss = unintentional decrease in value.

**Key principles**

- Insurance only covers pure risks.
- Every accident is an occurrence, but not every occurrence is an accident.
- Self-insurance is different from no insurance (planned vs. unplanned).
- Adverse selection works against the insurance company.

**Exam-day habits**

- Read carefully for key terms and qualifiers like "EXCEPT" or "NOT."
- Consider all elements of a concept before answering.
- Unsure about risk type? Ask "Can this result in a gain?"
- Insurance is based on uncertainty and chance.
