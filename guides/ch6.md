Basic medical pays first-dollar for a short list of services; major medical pays broadly after cost sharing; the ACA, HIPAA and tax-favored accounts set the rules around both. Everything here comes from the course screens except the memory hooks, which are labeled as made up.

## 1. Basic vs major medical

The two main types of medical expense insurance differ on deductibles, limits and how much they cover. Knowing what each covers and how it pays is a common test point.

| Feature | Basic medical expense | Major medical expense |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deductible | None: "first-dollar" coverage | Annual deductible, can be significant |
| Limits | Relatively modest limits or time frames | High maximum benefits; unlimited for ACA essential health benefits |
| What is covered | Only a specific list: if it is not listed, it is not covered | All medically necessary treatment unless explicitly excluded ("open-peril" basis, fewer exclusions) |
| Cost sharing | Benefits often lower than actual expenses | Deductibles, coinsurance and copays, capped by an out-of-pocket maximum |
| Payment basis | Indemnity (fixed dollar amount) or schedule | Based on the UCR standard (often with network pricing) |
| Types | Hospital, surgical, physician | Supplemental or comprehensive |
| ACA status | "Excepted benefits," exempt from ACA requirements | Meets ACA minimum essential coverage; renewable one-year term |

**Common exclusions from ALL medical expense policies:** elective cosmetic procedures, experimental therapies, care in government facilities, work-related injuries, and occupational diseases (covered by workers' compensation).

Basic medical can be bought for a range of benefits: emergency accident, maternity, mental and nervous disorders, hospice, critical care, home health care, outpatient care and treatment, convalescent care, nursing homes, dental and vision, and nurses' expenses including private duty nursing.

## 2. Basic policies: hospital, surgical, physician

Each basic policy pays for a different slice of a hospital stay: hospital pays for **things**, surgical and physician pay for **practitioners**.

| Policy | What it covers | How it pays |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic hospital expense | Room and board (room, general nursing care, food, hygiene items) plus miscellaneous expenses while confined: lab and X-ray, inpatient medicines, intensive care, operating room, surgical supplies | Indemnity basis: a fixed daily amount from a schedule, not tied to actual cost; no deductible; maximum on inpatient days and total paid |
| Basic surgical expense | Surgeons' services in or out of the hospital: surgeon's fees, anesthesiologist, operating room | Surgical schedule or relative value scale (most common); UCR more likely in a minimum essential coverage ("skinny") plan |
| Basic physician expense | A physician's non-surgical services (often called outpatient services) in the hospital, office or home (house call) | A fixed amount per visit (e.g., $50); usually included with a hospital or surgical plan, not written alone |

- Basic hospital covers expenses **billed directly by the hospital**. Except for nursing care, it covers things, not practitioners. Anyone who bills independently is covered by surgical or physician policies.
- Course example: in a surgery, the surgical policy covers the physicians; the hospital policy covers the operating theater, supplies and nursing staff.
- Course example: hospital benefit $200/day, hospital charges $400/day → insured pays the extra $200/day (works like hospital indemnity).
- Physician assistant policies cover physicians and other licensed providers within their range of practice. State statutes generally prohibit denying a claim because a physician assistant performed the service.

### Scheduled vs relative value scale vs UCR

| Approach | How benefits are set | When costs change | Where it is common |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Surgical schedule (scheduled plan) | A flat dollar amount for each listed procedure | Insurer must update the whole schedule | Basic surgical |
| Relative value scale (RVS) | Each surgery gets a number of units; each unit is worth a set dollar amount (the conversion factor) | Change only the dollar value per unit; change a procedure's units if it shifts relative to others | Basic surgical |
| Usual, customary and reasonable (UCR), non-scheduled | Compared to what is customary for the geographic region (the fee charged by 70% to 80% of practitioners); UCR is the most the insurer treats as eligible | Follows local fees | Comprehensive and major medical |

**RVS math (course example, scale = $100 per unit):** Appendectomy = 22 units × $100 = **$2,200** surgeon fee; anesthesiologist 4 units = **$400**. Spinal fusion = 60 units = **$6,000**; anesthesiologist 12 units = $1,200. These match the course's fixed-dollar sample schedule, so the same benefits can be set either way.

| Procedure | Surgeon (schedule / RVS units) | Anesthesiologist (schedule / RVS units) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appendectomy | $2,200 / 22 | $400 / 4 |
| Cesarean section | $3,200 / 32 | $600 / 6 |
| Knee arthroscopy | $2,600 / 26 | $500 / 5 |
| Hernia repair | $2,000 / 20 | $400 / 4 |
| Gallbladder removal | $3,000 / 30 | $600 / 6 |
| Tonsillectomy | $1,400 / 14 | $300 / 3 |
| Hysterectomy | $3,200 / 32 | $700 / 7 |
| Spinal fusion | $6,000 / 60 | $1,200 / 12 |

**UCR math (course example):** UCR for a procedure is $2,000 and the policy pays 80% of UCR.

1. Insurer pays 80% × $2,000 = **$1,600**; insured pays **$400**.
2. If the practitioner charges $2,400, the insurer still pays only **$1,600** (80% of UCR). The insured pays the remaining **$800**.

## 3. Major medical

Major medical's most basic job is protection against catastrophic medical expenses. It offers high maximum benefits and comprehensive coverage under one policy with a renewable one-year term.

- The ACA defined major medical as **minimum essential coverage (MEC)**. MEC is required to sell a plan on the exchange and to meet the ACA group mandate. Qualified plans cover at least **60%** of projected costs based on average use.
- Sold as group plans and individual policies through employers, commercial agents and government online marketplaces.
- Covers the 10 essential health benefits without limit (Section 6), plus hospice benefits (pain management, home-based services, counseling).
- Covers durable medical equipment (renting hospital wheelchairs, beds, casts, splints, trusses, braces, crutches) and prolonged illness or injury needing extended rehabilitation.
- Covers inpatient and outpatient hospital expenses. Because it works on an **"open-peril"** basis, it has fewer exclusions and fills the gaps left by basic plans.
- After the deductible, the insurer covers most costs; the insured pays modest copays or a coinsurance percentage. Once the insured hits the annual cost-sharing maximum, the insurer pays 100%. That annual limit is the **out-of-pocket maximum**.
- A policy may include some first-dollar basic benefits before the major medical deductible applies. A deductible that separates basic and major medical benefits is a **corridor deductible**.

### Supplemental vs comprehensive major medical

Major medical usually starts where basic coverage ends: it either supplements a basic plan or is part of one comprehensive policy.

| Feature | Supplemental major medical | Comprehensive major medical |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | A separate policy on top of a basic medical expense policy | Basic (first-dollar) + major medical combined and sold as a **single policy** |
| When it pays | After the basic policy pays its maximum; covers what basic excludes, amounts over basic's maximum, and care beyond basic's time limit | Covers nearly all medical expenses: hospital, physician, surgical, nursing, drugs, lab tests |
| Deductible | **Corridor deductible**: paid once the basic plan reaches its limit, falling between basic coverage and major medical | Typically a single deductible per person with a maximum family deductible |

### Lifetime and internal limits

- Major medical used to carry a lifetime maximum benefit, and some policies had annual maximums on certain benefits. The ACA prohibited annual and lifetime limits on **essential health benefits**.
- Policies may still put lifetime limits on a limited number of non-essential benefits. Grandfathered group plans (exempt from the ACA) may impose limits more liberally.
- **Internal (inside) limits** are annual limits on specific covered services. ACA-compliant plans may still limit costs to reasonable charges and set internal limits on non-essential benefits. Examples: semi-private room only, not private; usual and customary expenses only; lifetime alcohol or drug rehab up to $10,000 or 75 days.
- **Benefit period** begins either when the accident or illness occurs or when the insured meets the deductible. Some end each year; others last up to two years.

### Pre-existing conditions

- The ACA prohibits pre-existing condition exclusions in compliant major medical plans. They still exist in short-term medical policies that fill coverage gaps, and may apply to some limited medical expense policies and Medicare-related policies.
- Traditionally, insurers rated applicants with disclosed conditions or excluded them permanently with an **impairment rider**.
- Policies that are not medically underwritten use a **blanket pre-existing condition exclusion** for any condition needing care immediately before the policy starts. It lasts **six months to two years**, depending on policy type and regulations.

## 4. Deductibles

A deductible is what the insured pays before the policy pays. Deductibles control premium costs and reduce overutilization: because the insured keeps part of the risk, the insurer can offer high protection at a lower premium.

**Cost sharing** = copays, deductibles and coinsurance (the policy's out-of-pocket costs). NOT cost sharing: premiums, penalties, or bills for services the policy does not cover.

| Deductible type | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Calendar-year (cumulative, all-cause) | **Most common.** Met only once per benefit period, whatever the cause |
| Carryover provision | Deductible not met by the final 3 months of the policy year → claims paid in those 3 months count toward next year's deductible |
| Flat (initial) | A stated dollar amount (e.g., $500). Can be per occurrence (called the "initial deductible"), per insured, or per year. Generally applies to each person insured |
| Per-cause (per-occurrence) | A separate deductible for each accident or illness |
| Common accident or sickness | Only ONE deductible when 2+ family members are hurt in the same accident or have the same illness at the same time; usually equals the individual deductible |
| Family maximum | Caps the total the whole family pays, usually 2 or 3 times the individual deductible |
| Corridor | The gap between basic coverage and major medical; not applied until basic coverage is exhausted |
| Integrated | Major medical packaged with basic (or any policies sharing one deductible): amounts paid by the basic policy count toward the major medical deductible |

### Family deductible example (course)

Without a family cap, a $1,000 individual deductible could cost a two-person family $2,000 and a family of five $5,000. Now give the family of five a **$1,000 individual** and **$2,000 family** deductible:

- Father $800 + mother $600 + two oldest children $300 each + youngest $0 = **$2,000**.
- Nobody met the individual deductible, but the family deductible is met, so the insurer pays the next covered claim.
- If one member meets the individual deductible first, only that member's claims are paid until the family meets the rest of the family deductible.

### Integrated deductible examples (course)

- Major medical with a $500 deductible plus basic coverage of $500 or more: the basic payment satisfies the major medical deductible. If basic pays less than $500, the insured makes up the difference.
- A medical policy and a prescription drug plan with a $500 integrated deductible: one $500 payment satisfies both.

### Flat vs corridor vs integrated: John's $7,000 claim (course)

John is hospitalized at the start of his policy period with **$7,000** of covered expenses. His major medical deductible is **$5,000**. In versions 2 and 3, a basic policy also pays the first **$4,000**.

| $7,000 total expenses | 1. No basic policy (flat deductible) | 2. Basic + corridor deductible | 3. Basic + integrated deductible |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Basic policy pays | N/A | $4,000 | $4,000 |
| John pays | $5,000 | $3,000 | $1,000 |
| Major medical pays | $2,000 | N/A | $2,000 |
| Total covered by insurance | $2,000 | $4,000 | $6,000 |

- **Version 2:** basic pays $4,000, leaving $3,000. John owes up to $5,000 in the corridor deductible, so he pays all $3,000.
- **Version 3:** the major medical credits basic's $4,000 toward the $5,000 deductible. John pays the last $1,000, and major medical pays the remaining $2,000.

## 5. Coinsurance and stop-loss

Always work out the insured's share first; the insurer's share is the total minus that.

- **Coinsurance** ("percentage participation") is the share of covered expenses the insured pays after the deductible, written insurer/insured (80/20 = insurer 80%, insured 20% plus the deductible). Its purpose is to reduce overutilization (unnecessary care). It applies for the life of the policy.
- Most major medical plans (other than HMOs, which limit out-of-network care to emergencies) pay **70% to 90%** in-network and **at least 60%** out-of-network.
- The insurer must pay **at least 50%** for the plan to count as "insurance" rather than a "discount plan."
- **Stop-loss** originally meant the most coinsurance an insured paid before the insurer took 100% for the rest of the policy period. Today it means the **out-of-pocket maximum**.
- The out-of-pocket maximum INCLUDES the deductible, coinsurance and copays. It does NOT include premiums, charges above usual and customary, or out-of-network services the plan does not cover.

### The five calculation steps (course review notes)

1. Determine the total claim amount.
2. Subtract the deductible.
3. Apply the coinsurance percentage to the remainder.
4. Add the deductible and coinsurance = the insured's total cost.
5. Apply the stop-loss, if any.

For the insurer's share (course drag-and-drop): total claim → subtract the deductible → multiply by the insurer's percentage (e.g., 80%).

### Worked example 1: $1,500 claim, $500 deductible, 80/20

1. $1,500 − $500 deductible = $1,000 left.
2. Insured's coinsurance: 20% × $1,000 = $200.
3. Insured pays $500 + $200 = **$700**.
4. Insurer pays $1,500 − $700 = **$800** (check: 80% × $1,000 = $800).

### Worked example 2: John's $30,000 claim, $5,000 deductible, 70/30, ACA stop-loss of $9,200 (2025)

| $30,000 total expenses | Without stop-loss | With $9,200 stop-loss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deductible paid by John | $5,000 | $5,000 |
| Left for coinsurance | $25,000 | $25,000 |
| 70/30 split (insurer / John) | $17,500 / $7,500 | $17,500 / $7,500 |
| Apply stop-loss | N/A | $12,500 − $9,200 = $3,300 shifted to the insurer |
| John's total for the year | $5,000 + $7,500 = **$12,500** | **$9,200** |

### Worked example 3: Sarah's $40,000 claim (course case study)

Sarah, 42, had a ruptured appendix: emergency surgery, a 7-day stay including 2 days in ICU, and complications. No earlier claims this year. Her bill: ER $2,800, imaging $4,200, lab $1,800, surgery and anesthesia $12,500, ICU $8,600, regular room $7,500, IV antibiotics and drugs $2,100, follow-up $500 = **$40,000**. Policy: $1,000 annual deductible, 80/20.

| Step | With $3,000 stop-loss | Without stop-loss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Apply the deductible | $40,000 − $1,000 = $39,000 | $40,000 − $1,000 = $39,000 |
| 2. Apply the coinsurance | $39,000 × 20% = $7,800 | $39,000 × 20% = $7,800 |
| 3. Insured's cost (deductible + coinsurance) | $1,000 + $7,800 = $8,800 | $1,000 + $7,800 = $8,800 |
| 4. Stop-loss effect | $8,800 − $3,000 = $5,800 shifted to insurer | N/A |
| 5. Total patient cost | **$3,000** | **$8,800** |
| 6. Insurer pays | $40,000 − $3,000 = **$37,000** | $40,000 − $8,800 = **$31,200** |

In both of the course's stop-loss examples, the stop-loss is the insured's total cap for the year, deductible included.

## 6. The Affordable Care Act (ACA)

The ACA, officially the **Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (PPACA)**, set federal minimum standards for major medical policies. It was signed in 2010 (also called "Obamacare"), and its major provisions took effect in 2014.

- Insurers must spend **at least 80%** of premium dollars on health care, not administration.
- Community rating limits how much premiums can vary by age (**3:1 ratio**).
- Mandates 10 essential health benefit categories, including preventive care without cost sharing.
- No denial and no higher premiums for pre-existing conditions; no lifetime or annual caps on essential health benefits.
- Expanded Medicaid eligibility, subsidized premiums, gave businesses incentives to offer benefits, and supported medical research. Paid for by taxes and cost savings (e.g., fairer Medicare Advantage payments relative to traditional Medicare).
- Created state and federal exchanges (marketplaces) for the individual and small-group markets. Only qualified health benefit plans can be sold there.

### Employer rules and subsidies

| Provision | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Employer shared responsibility payment (ESRP) | Employers with **at least 50** full-time employees (or equivalents) must offer affordable, minimum-value coverage to full-time employees and dependents, or face tax penalties |
| Small employer tax credit | Employers with **fewer than 25** full-time-equivalent employees that provide coverage may get a tax credit for part of the premiums |
| Advance premium tax credit (APTC) | Federal tax credit that lowers Marketplace premiums for income **up to 400%** of the federal poverty level; in most states, 100% to 400% of FPL get subsidies |
| SHOP exchange | Small Business Health Options Program, open to employers with **50 or fewer** full-time-equivalent employees |

**The exchange** is a federal website (the government calls it the "marketplace") to check eligibility for government help, compare individual and small-group plans, and buy coverage. It was created to reduce the uninsured, make it easier to buy qualified plans, help employers enroll employees, and help people reach public programs, premium tax credits and cost-sharing reductions. Its roles: certify plans as qualified, present plan options in a uniform format, and verify and resolve inconsistent applicant information.

### Enrollment periods

| Period | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Open enrollment (OEP) | Generally **November 1 to January 15**. Enroll before December 16 → coverage starts January 1; December 16 to January 15 → starts February 1 |
| Special enrollment (SEP) | Needs a qualifying event ("change in status"): loss of job-based coverage, marriage, birth or adoption, divorce, moving. **60-day** window (90 days for loss of Medicaid); documentation required. Begins 60 days BEFORE the old plan ends, so there is no gap |

Loss of minimum essential coverage is a qualifying event. Coverage can end when the insurer leaves the marketplace, the person leaves a job (group coverage), divorce or legal separation, or death. The person can then enroll on or off the exchange.

### Metal tiers

Each tier must cover at least a set share (**actuarial value**) of expected essential health benefit costs. The rest is what the insured expects to pay through deductibles, copays and out-of-pocket limits.

| Tier | Actuarial value | Insured pays | Premiums and deductibles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bronze | 60% (60/40) | 40% | Low premiums, high deductibles |
| Silver | 70% (70/30) | 30% | Moderate premiums and deductibles |
| Gold | 80% (80/20) | 20% | Higher premiums, lower deductibles |
| Platinum | 90% (90/10) | 10% | Highest premiums, lowest deductibles |

All marketplace tiers share the same out-of-pocket maximum. After it is reached, the plan pays 100% of covered benefits. **2025: $9,200 individual / $18,400 family.** It excludes monthly premiums and non-covered services, and it is adjusted every year.

### The 10 essential health benefits (no lifetime or annual cap)

1. **Ambulatory patient services:** care without being admitted (walk-in clinic, doctor's office, same-day surgery center, ER visit without admission).
2. **Emergency services:** conditions that could cause severe disability or death if not treated right away.
3. **Hospitalization:** inpatient care: room and board, nursing, physician, diagnostic tests, drugs during the stay.
4. **Pregnancy, maternity and newborn care.**
5. **Prescription drugs:** for acute illness (an infection) or ongoing conditions (high blood pressure).
6. **Mental health and substance use disorder services,** including behavioral health treatment.
7. **Rehabilitative and habilitative services and devices:** help to gain or recover mental and physical skills (habilitative = skills for everyday living).
8. **Laboratory services:** tests of blood, tissue and other samples.
9. **Preventive and wellness services and chronic disease management** (physicals, screenings, immunizations; asthma or diabetes management).
10. **Pediatric services, including oral (dental) and vision care.** Children's vision follows AAP and American Association for Pediatric Ophthalmology guidance: screenings, a comprehensive eye exam, and refraction for children who fail a screening.

Limited pediatric dental and vision are **mandatory**; adult dental and vision are **optional** and usually cost extra.

### Preventive care at 100% (no copay, deductible or coinsurance)

- Mammograms for breast cancer annually or every two years for women over 40
- Pap smears every three years for women 21 and older
- Pre- and post-natal care: folic acid, gestational diabetes screening, breastfeeding support and equipment, certain immunizations
- All FDA-approved contraception, with counseling and education
- Domestic violence screening and counseling; HIV screening
- Genetic testing for breast cancer genes for high-risk women; approved clinical trials

### Dependents

- Eligible: a spouse who is not legally separated, and married or unmarried children **up to age 26** (natural, adopted and stepchildren). Longer if the child is a disabled dependent.
- New dependents from marriage, birth or adoption are covered from the date they became dependents.
- COBRA lets a child who "ages out" of a group plan continue that coverage for up to **36 months**.

### Grandfathered plans vs skinny (MEC) plans

|  | Grandfathered plan | Skinny plan (MEC plan) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Existed in its current form before the ACA became law on **March 23, 2010** | Low-cost, limited-benefit plan that technically meets ACA minimum essential coverage |
| Catch | Loses status if it raises deductibles, coinsurance or other cost sharing | Usually only preventive and wellness care; little or no hospitalization, emergency or expensive treatment |
| ACA rules | NOT required to cover free preventive care, essential health benefits, or pre-existing conditions in the individual market. Still cannot cap the essential benefits it does cover | Provides MEC but NOT **minimum value** (covers at least 60% of total costs plus substantial inpatient and physician coverage) |
| Notes |  | Used by employers (restaurants, hotels, retail) to avoid penalties. Employees may qualify for Marketplace tax credits; gaps can be filled with supplemental or hospital-only policies |

## 7. HIPAA

HIPAA keeps people from losing coverage or facing a new pre-existing condition period when they change or lose a job. The course calls it the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act **of 1997**, passed more than a decade before the ACA.

- It treated everyone covered under all group plans as **one underwriting pool** for insurability. Someone with creditable coverage can move to another carrier without new-participant restrictions.
- Key provisions: limits on what counts as pre-existing, portability of coverage, and expanded COBRA eligibility.
- Applies only to **groups of two or more**, not individual policies. It also created privacy rules (the **HIPAA Privacy Rule** protects personal health information and gives patients rights over it).

### Portability

- Portability does NOT let you carry a specific policy from job to job. It lets you use proof of prior coverage to shorten or remove a new plan's pre-existing condition exclusion.
- Applies to nearly all group plans with at least two participants on the first day of the plan year.
- Employers must issue a **certificate of creditable coverage** on request. The employee and family qualify if there was no gap of **63 days or more** (the gap can be at most 62 days).
- Each month of creditable coverage cancels one month of the pre-existing waiting period. **12 months** of prior coverage = no pre-existing exclusion at all.
- Uninsured for **63+ days** between jobs = no HIPAA protection: the full pre-existing period applies.

### Pre-existing condition rules

| Rule | HIPAA limit |
| --- | --- |
| Look-back period | Only conditions treated, advised on, cared for or diagnosed in the **6 months** before joining the new group plan |
| Maximum exclusion period | **12 months**; **18 months** for a late enrollee; reduced by creditable coverage |
| Never pre-existing | Undiagnosed conditions later diagnosed; maternity; births; adoptions |
| Newborns and adoptions | Must be added to the plan within **30 days** of birth or adoption |
| Creditable coverage | Group plans, individual plans, Medicare, Medicaid, other state and federal programs. It need not come from a single employer |

Course example: Dan was covered by his employer from March 1, 2023 to April 5, 2024, then joined a grandfathered group plan. He had more than 12 months of coverage, so no pre-existing exclusion can apply, even under a grandfathered plan.

## 8. Taxation and tax-advantaged accounts

Benefits received from a medical expense policy are **not taxable**: they offset expenses, so they are not income. Whether premiums and costs are deductible depends on who pays.

| Taxpayer | Premium and expense deductibility |
| --- | --- |
| Employees who itemize | Medical and dental expenses (after-tax premiums, coinsurance, deductibles, some uncovered costs) are deductible only above **7.5% of AGI**. No deduction for expenses insurance reimbursed or for pre-tax benefit contributions |
| Self-employed and sole proprietors | **100%** of health premiums (including spouse and children) deductible as a business expense; other medical costs follow the 7.5% of AGI rule |
| Partnerships and LLCs | Premiums paid by the partnership are deductible to the partnership or LLC |

**7.5% of AGI math (course example):** Jane's AGI is $100,000, so the threshold is 7.5% × $100,000 = $7,500. She has $8,500 of medical expenses. Deductible amount = $8,500 − $7,500 = **$1,000**.

### Consumer-driven health plans (CDHPs)

A CDHP has three elements: (1) a tax-advantaged savings vehicle (HSA, HRA or Archer MSA), (2) a corridor or integrated deductible, and (3) a qualifying high-deductible policy. The account pays for care and deductibles with pre-tax money, the deductible gap makes people weigh costs, and the major medical caps the risk.

### HSA vs HRA vs FSA vs Archer MSA

| Feature | HSA | HRA | FSA | Archer MSA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full name | Health savings account | Health reimbursement arrangement | Flexible spending arrangement | Archer medical savings account |
| Who sets it up and funds it | The individual owns it; individual and/or employer contribute (pre-tax via a cafeteria plan) | Employer establishes and funds it | Employee sets aside earnings through the employer's cafeteria plan | Employer OR employee, but only one of them in a given year |
| HDHP required? | Yes | Yes (used with HDHPs) | **No** | Yes |
| Contribution limit | IRS limit (see numbers below) | **No limit** | IRS limit (see below) | A percentage of the deductible (75% in the 2026 table) |
| Unused money | Rolls over and accumulates | Carries forward | **"Use it or lose it"** (small rollover allowed: $640 in 2025); employer cannot refund unused funds | Rolls over |
| Portable? | **Yes**: employee owns funds and account | **No**: balance stays with employer | No (use it or lose it) | Rolls over; program closed to new participants |
| Non-qualified withdrawals | Income tax + **20%** penalty | Only approved expenses reimbursed (tax-free) | Qualified medical expenses only | **20%** tax penalty |
| Special notes | Created by the Medicare Prescription Drug and Modernization Act of 2003 | Employer gives a credit, never transfers cash | Can't have one with an HSA or Archer MSA (an HRA is OK) | Small employers (≤ 50 employees) and self-employed; pilot for today's HSA; only grandfathered accounts since Jan 1, 2008 |

### HSA details

- **Eligible:** covered by an HDHP; no other medical expense coverage (e.g., a basic surgical plan), except ACA free preventive care. Accident, disability, dental, vision and long-term care coverage are fine. Not eligible for Medicare (so generally under 65). Cannot be claimed as someone else's dependent.
- **Tax treatment:** contributions are tax-deductible; earnings grow tax-free; withdrawals for qualified expenses are tax-free. On death, it can pass tax-free to a spouse.
- **Qualified expenses:** doctors' fees, prescription and non-prescription medicines, hospital services insurance did not pay, retiree health premiums, Medicare expenses (but **not Medigap**), qualified long-term care, COBRA premiums. Covers the owner, spouse and dependents.
- **Leaving an HDHP:** can still spend the money, but CANNOT contribute until covered by a qualifying HDHP again.
- Limits change each year, effective January 1. The **$1,000** age-55 catch-up has not changed in over a decade.

**HDHP:** a major medical plan where the insured pays basic expenses (other than ACA preventive care) up to an annual out-of-pocket cap. The IRS sets the minimum deductible and maximum out-of-pocket for an HDHP to qualify for an HSA.

### Medicare MSA

A type of Medicare Advantage (Part C) plan: a high-deductible Medicare Advantage plan plus an MSA that Medicare deposits money into each year. Any Medicare-approved provider who accepts Medicare can be used; unused money stays. You **cannot** join if other coverage would pay the MSA deductible, you're in another Medicare Advantage plan, you get TRICARE or VA benefits, you're a retired federal employee with FEHBP, you're eligible for Medicaid, you're in hospice, or you live outside the U.S. more than 183 days a year.

### Cafeteria plans (Section 125)

- A Section 125 (Internal Revenue Code) plan lets U.S. businesses offer a menu of benefits, including accident and health insurance, on a **pre-tax** basis. CDHPs and FSAs are often offered through one.
- Sole proprietors, partners, and S-corporation shareholders owning **2% or more** cannot participate or get participant tax benefits (they can take the plan sponsor's deductions). C-corporation shareholders can participate regardless of ownership.

## 11. Exam Tips from the course

1. **What they cover and how they pay:** focus on what each medical expense policy covers and how it pays benefits; these are common test points.
2. **Integrated deductible:** when the deductible is integrated, amounts paid by basic insurance offset the deductible.
3. **Coinsurance math:** whatever the question asks, calculate the insured's amount first. If it asks for the insurer's cost, subtract the insured's portion from the total claim. It is simpler, with fewer steps.
4. **Child age-out:** watch for child "age-out" questions on the state exam. Note whether the question is about state law, ACA requirements, a student, a handicapped child, and so on.

**End-of-chapter review tips:**

- Know the differences between basic and major medical policies.
- Understand how to calculate deductibles, coinsurance and stop-loss.
- Memorize the 10 essential health benefits required by the ACA.
- Know the characteristics and limitations of tax-advantaged accounts.
- Remember HDHP minimum deductibles and maximum out-of-pocket limits.
- Understand HIPAA portability provisions and pre-existing condition rules.
- Know the four metal tiers and their actuarial values.

## 12. Memory hooks (made up, not from the course)

- **"Hospital pays for THINGS, doctors pay for PEOPLE."** Basic hospital = room, supplies, operating room, nurses. Surgical and physician = practitioners who bill on their own.
- **Schedule = Sticker price, RVS = Points × price, UCR = what the neighbors charge.** RVS: units × $ per unit.
- **Corridor = a hallway you walk yourself.** Basic stops, you pay through the corridor, then major medical starts. Integrated = basic's payments count toward your deductible.
- **Metal tiers go up by tens: 6-7-8-9.** Bronze 60, Silver 70, Gold 80, Platinum 90. What you pay is the rest: 40-30-20-10.
- **HIPAA's "6-12-18-63":** 6-month look-back, 12-month max exclusion, 18 for late enrollees, a 63-day gap breaks it.
- **HSA = "Has Savings Always"**: you own it, it rolls over, it goes with you. **HRA = "Held by the Real Agency"** (employer): no limit, not portable. **FSA = "Forfeit Soon Anyway"**: use it or lose it, no HDHP needed.
- **The 10 EHBs: "A Hospital Pregnancy Requires Mental Rehab, Lab, Prevention, Pediatrics, and Emergency care."** (Ambulatory, Hospitalization, Pregnancy, Rx, Mental health, Rehab, Lab, Preventive, Pediatric, Emergency.)
- **Math order: D-C-A-S.** Deductible off, Coinsurance on the rest, Add them, Stop-loss cap.

## 14. Answer key

| # | Answer | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | B | Basic hospital covers things billed by the hospital, including nursing staff. The anesthesiologist and attending physician bill on their own (surgical / physician policies) |
| 2 | D | Deductibles and coinsurance belong to major medical. Basic is first-dollar |
| 3 | C | 26 units × $100 = $2,600 |
| 4 | C | Insurer pays 80% × $2,000 UCR = $1,600. Insured pays $2,400 − $1,600 = $800 |
| 5 | B | Basic's $4,000 counts toward the $5,000 integrated deductible; insured pays the last $1,000; major medical pays $2,000 |
| 6 | C | Basic pays $4,000, leaving $3,000; the insured owes up to $5,000 in the corridor, so pays all $3,000 |
| 7 | B | Insured: $500 + 20% × $2,500 = $1,000. Insurer: $3,000 − $1,000 = $2,000 |
| 8 | C | Insured would owe $1,000 + 20% × $24,000 = $5,800, capped at $3,000. Insurer: $25,000 − $3,000 = $22,000 |
| 9 | B | $250 × 4 = $1,000 meets the family deductible even though no one met the individual one |
| 10 | C | The carryover provision covers the final three months of the policy year |
| 11 | C | Gold = 80% actuarial value, so the insured pays about 20% |
| 12 | B | Pediatric dental and vision are mandatory; adult dental and vision are optional |
| 13 | B | Each month of creditable coverage cancels a month: 12 − 8 = 4 months |
| 14 | C | Medicare expenses qualify, but Medigap does not |
| 15 | D | Unlike the other accounts, an FSA does not require an HDHP |
| 16 | B | 7.5% × $60,000 = $4,500 threshold; $6,000 − $4,500 = $1,500 |
